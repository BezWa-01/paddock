02

The pre-start, in order

Everything here happens before the first pass, most of it with the engine off. Skip it and the automation inherits a badly set machine — which is the single most common way a $1M combine underperforms a well-driven older one.

A · Out of the combine

  • Both Safety Wing Lock Ball Valves unlocked — vertical. Both Wing Manual Override Valves rotated clockwise to the harvesting position.
  • Knife hold-down clearance: 0.50 mm under each hold-down — a business card. Apply light down pressure on the section next to the 10 mm adjuster.
  • Knife sections centred on the rock guards. Inspect for broken or dull sections, worn guard edges, binding at the guard slot. Fine tooth sections for wheat, barley, oats and canola.
  • Spin the complete cutting system by hand with the drive shaft removed. It must turn freely.
  • Reel-to-cutterbar clearance, wings down and head in full frown: 15 mm at the hinge, 45 mm at the outer float arms. Set reel finger pitch first — changing pitch moves this clearance.
  • Reel finger pitch to the middle position as a start. Least aggressive for tall standing canola; most aggressive only for down or tangled crop.
  • Feed drum finger timing: centre section in neutral, spin the drum to the lowest finger — gap to the feed floor 40 mm. Handle on the RH side of the drum.
  • Top auger stripper to flight clearance 5 mm, most important at the centre. Fingers fully retracted as they cross the stripper.
  • Side and centre draper belt tension — indicator centred on the gauge, both sides of each belt. Re-check after the first 10 minutes if you de-tensioned for service.
  • Feeder house conveyor chain tension 18–25 mm. Remove half links as it stretches; replace when the idler reaches the end of the slot.
  • Feeder house sprocket, feed accelerator gear, rotor gear and concave set to the crop you are actually starting in — see section 03.
  • Concaves zeroed and levelled front to rear. Out-of-level concaves create a pinch point and damage grain.
  • Separator grate spacers in the storage position for all small grains, both rotors matched. Deflector shields reinstalled.
  • Measure chaffer and sieve openings and confirm they match the cab display, left and right. If they do not, run the factory calibration.
  • Front chaffer at 25 mm. Every crop, every time. Closing it nozzles the airflow and costs capacity.
  • Chopper in high, counter knife engaged to 50% as a start.

B · In the cab, before you engage

  • Correct crop type selected. Deere's suggested defaults pull starting settings from the fleet database — use them as a start, not a destination.
  • All Automatic Header Height controls ON: Height Sensing, Height Resume, Lateral Tilt, Fore/Aft Resume, Reel Position Resume, Auto Reel Speed, Auto Belt Speed. The HDR50 is hinged — Height Sensing must be on at all times or the wings will not work.
  • Ground Conditions set. Start Typical. Softer settings make the suspension more rigid; they are stored against the button you save them to.
  • Draper belt speed start at 75. Back shaft 520 rpm.
  • Programme the hydro handle: button 2 = normal off-ground harvest configuration; button 3 = down-crop configuration (on ground, gauge wheels retracted, cutterbar angle more aggressive, reel down and forward). Hold button 2 for five seconds to save fore/aft tilt and reel position.
  • Mass flow vibration calibration with the grain tank empty, at operating speed — once for the HDR50 and again for the BP15. Two headers, two calibrations.
  • Moisture sensor calibrated with the bypass empty and the sensor clean.
  • Grain loss display algorithm set to Area-based (the X9 default). Time-based and mass-flow modes are erratic on headlands and in light crop.
  • Fields, boundaries, headlands, guidance tracks and work plans already in Operations Center and synced. Machine and header offsets and width correct — every yield map you build this season depends on it.
  • Machine Sync home points set. Grain cart tractor at full throttle, set speed roughly double harvest speed.
  • Acceptance limits for grain loss, foreign material and broken grain not yet set. Those come after the tray drop, not before.
First 200 metres, every crop and every paddock change

Harvest a short distance, then do a power shutdown and walk the machine. Check the header-to-feeder-house connection, stone trap seals, shoe seals, and that the auger trough and grain tank clean-out are closed. Then look at the chaffer: if the chaff is sitting above the bay dividers, the shoe is overloaded — back off the threshing aggression or fit separator grate covers before you touch fan or sieve. Air takes the path of least resistance; an uneven chaff load will cost you grain out the back and a dirty tank at the same time.

Setting the machine, crop by crop

These are Deere's published starting points for the X Series, not finishing points. Moisture, variety and time of day move every one of them. Pick the column closest to what you have, set it, then work the loss.

Two places Deere's own documents disagree — decide before harvest

Active Tailings in canola. The Ready-To-Harvest canola checklist says set the lever up, to the Corn (open) position. The crop-settings page in the optimisation guide says Small Grains (closed). Both are right for different canola: open for dry, brittle, easy-threshing conventional canola where you want gentle handling and less chaff load; closed for shatter-resistant or tough canola that needs re-threshing. Decide per paddock with the pod squeeze test, not per season.

The 15T / 18T sprocket in windrowed canola. The "Harvesting Swathed Canola" page calls it the feeder house drive sprocket. The BP15 manual is unambiguous: 15T and 18T are the BP15 auger drive (181 rpm and 218 rpm at 520 rpm back shaft). The X9 feeder house conveyor chain uses 18T and 22T. Do not let a new operator pull the wrong sprocket off.

04

Finding, splitting and pricing the loss

Five places grain goes missing. Only three of them are the combine's fault, and the WA survey found the other two are usually bigger. If you cannot say which of the five you are looking at, every setting change is a guess.

The three-sample method

Deere's in-cab loss bars cannot separate front loss from machine loss, and front loss is the bigger number in most WA crops. Three quadrats, one stop, five minutes — and you know which half of the machine to work on.

The arithmetic, once

By count

Loss in kg/ha = seeds per m² × TGW(g) ÷ 100

Count into a 0.1 m² frame (a 500 × 200 mm timber square is ideal) and multiply the count by 10 to get seeds per m². Counting beats weighing in canola and lupins; weighing beats counting in cereals.

By weight

Loss in kg/ha = grams in quadrat ÷ quadrat area(m²) × 10

Aspirate or winnow the sample first — chaff in the pan reads as grain and will send you chasing a problem you do not have. A $40 kitchen scale reading to 0.1 g is enough.

The rule this produces — put it on the cab card

Drop a tray on every crop change and every front change, in the first hour. Not on day three. The 14 November test cost a real amount (withheld) to find a problem that had been running for three days; dropped on the afternoon of the 11th it would have cost a few hundred. X9 #1 changed crop and front on the same afternoon and ran 2,877 ha worth of program behind a concave set for oats.

Both machines, not one. The two X9s do not converge on the same settings on their own — across the settled canola period #1 sat at 36 mm median concave and #2 at 31 mm, and in wheat they ran 9 mm apart. A test on one machine tells you nothing reliable about the other.

Then anchor the bars. Once the tray says the machine is right, press Set to current. That is what turns the in-cab display from decoration into a working alarm for the rest of that paddock — and it is the step that was missing on X9 #1 between the 11th and the 14th.

Three things that make a tray number trustworthy

1. Aspirate the catch. This is the one that matters most in canola. Chaff and pod fragments are light and bulky, and an uncleaned catch can be most of the weight. The arithmetic is linear on a 1 m pan in a 3 t/ha crop: 9 g of clean seed is 1%, 27 g is 3%, 45 g is 5%, 91 g is 10%. A 120 g catch that is only a quarter seed is a 3.3% test, not a 13.2% one — and the two call for completely different responses.

2. Eyeball the ground before you trust the pan. 397 kg/ha is about 113 canola seeds in a 10 × 10 cm square — a visible carpet, not a scatter. Thirty seconds on your knees behind the machine tells you whether the pan and the paddock agree.

3. Enter the real spread width. A 1 m band is only true in Total Loss Mode — chopper raised and in neutral, straw curtain in. Spreading normally, a 1 m pan catches a slice of a much wider pattern and the entry has to be the actual spread width, or the number comes out low.

One smaller thing: the canola screen carries a combine capacity of 29 t/hr and the wheat screen 64.1 t/hr. Your measured season rates were 20.4 t/hr in canola and 43.7 t/hr in wheat. Those entries only drive the $/hr line, not the kg/ha, so nothing above is affected — but if you want the $/hr figure to mean anything, use the throughput the machine was actually running when the pan dropped.

What the in-cab loss bars are actually telling you

They are a change detector, not a measurement, and every operator who treats them as a measurement gets burned. When you press Set to current, the system anchors the display to whatever loss you happen to have at that instant, and a full display means roughly twice that. Anchor it at 0.5 units of loss and a full bar means 1.0 — anchor it at a bad number and the bars will happily read comfortable while the tray says otherwise.

  • Only the centre bar tracks relative loss on the ground. The shoe and separator bars tell you where the loss is coming from, which is the more useful half.
  • Keep the algorithm on Area-based. It uses header width and ground speed and is the only mode that correlates with what you find on the ground across a range of speeds.
  • The sequence that works: optimise the machine manually → drop the tray and get a real number → then press Set to current → from that point the bars hold the setting for you.
  • Watch t/hr, not ground speed. Feedrate is what drives loss, and it moves as yield changes underneath you while the speedo does not.

The automation stack

Four systems, one loop. They are genuinely good, and they will hold a well-set machine through a shifting afternoon better than a tired operator will. What they cannot do is discover that your baseline was wrong — they optimise toward the target you hand them.

Setting the performance target — the one thing to get right

Do this

  • Optimise the machine manually and confirm with a tray drop before pressing Set Performance Target.
  • Acquire on level ground, at high idle, above 0.25 km/h, header below recording height, with minimal stopping and starting. Allow 5–15 minutes; higher throughput acquires faster.
  • Temporarily turn Active Terrain Adjustment off while acquiring — if it is commanding changes it slows acquisition.
  • Re-set the target whenever yield, moisture, variety or crop type changes. It stores one target per crop.
  • In canola, raise Response Aggressiveness — it is driven by throughput, and canola's low t/hr otherwise makes the system adjust too slowly.
  • In canola, raise the grain loss issue sensitivity and lower broken grain and foreign material. You are chasing loss, not a showroom sample.

Do not do this

  • Set the target in lodged crop, on a slope, or while the feedrate is bouncing. Deere's own line: bad target = bad performance.
  • Set acceptance limits tighter than your grain buyer needs. Deere states plainly that limits set too low will cost you productivity.
  • Assume the target survives the paddock change. It does not.
  • Trust the shortcut LED as a health check on every sensor — from MY25.5 it shows the Decide status, so a single faulted sensor can sit behind a green light.
  • Engage Ground Speed Automation straight after a battery cycle or a crop type change without reading the notice — it runs in limited performance while it relearns.

HarvestSmart / Ground Speed Automation

Cruise control for feedrate. It holds an engine power target using engine power, rotor load and loss sensors, and it is the single biggest fatigue reducer on a long day. Engage it once you are into the crop and loaded up — about 30 metres in — not on the way in.

How it follows the ground — and why that matters

The HDR uses the gauge wheels as its sole height input. The distance from wheel bottom to cutterbar is fixed; if the wheels lift, the knife lifts with them. The lift cylinder does not actively control height — it is not a 700D. So cut height is set from the cab by moving the wheels, and ground following quality is entirely a function of how much weight is on those wheels.

Down force

  • Start at Typical, even on hard soil.
  • Heavier for aggressive rolling terrain, high ground speed, or when the head is not moving fast enough to follow.
  • Lighter for wet or soft ground, and for rocks. If the cutterbar is pushing, go lighter before you do anything else.
  • Header raising out of the cut? Run a more firm Ground Condition setting.
  • Leave the Advanced Tuning page alone unless the dealer is on the phone. It is easy to make the head perform very poorly from there.

Reel — where canola loss is made

The reel's only job is to assist the crop over the cutterbar onto the side belts. Run it up and out, and match reel speed to ground speed. A reel running too fast is the single most common cause of header loss in canola: it threshes pods in the air over the knife and that seed is gone before the machine ever sees it.

The exception is shatter-resistant canola that is still wet and heavy — there you can run the reel much more aggressively, close to how you would run it in wheat, to keep the mat feeding. Tall, dry, non-shatter-resistant canola is the opposite: reel up and back, slow, fingers just brushing.

Attachments that pay in your crops

  • Centre section seal kit — purpose-built to stop pod-shatter loss at the centre when direct cutting canola. Remove the left and right seal components again for cereals.
  • Grain saver draper belts — side belts designed to stop header loss in shatter-prone crops.
  • Back sheet extensions — extra coverage for bulky canola, stops seed going over the back of the head.
  • Fingered top augers — leverage in bushy canola; time the fingers fully retracted at the stripper or they wrap.
  • Gauge wheel scrapers — mud on the wheels means a wrong cut height, and the head has no other height reference.
Two feed settings that change per crop, not per season

Centre feed two-speed: high for cereals and bulky canola; low for dry canola prone to shatter, because the feed drum itself will pre-thresh pods in the header. Feed drum finger timing: rotate the handle clockwise for more aggressive fingers in low-volume crops (dryland wheat, lentils), counter-clockwise for less aggressive in bulky crops (canola). Both take under a minute and both are routinely left where the last crop had them.

Setting the knife hold-downs

The one front adjustment that is both quick and routinely wrong. Hold-down clearance is, in Deere’s words, one of the most important factors in cutting tough straw — keeping the knife tight to the guard is what cuts cleanly at low power, and a loose knife rips stems and shakes seed out before the machine ever sees it.

  • Centre the knife sections on the rock guards.
  • Slide a 0.50 mm (0.020 in) feeler gauge under the hold-down. A business card works.
  • Apply light down pressure on the knife section beside the adjustment screw (10 mm bolt head).
  • Tighten until there is light pressure on the gauge or card.
  • Run the header 2–3 minutes, shut down, and feel each hold-down by hand. Hot to the touch means too tight — back it off.
  • In tough conditions reduce the clearance, but never below 0.30 mm (0.012 in), and only as much as the cut needs. Over-tightening costs knife life.

Data — closing the loop

Harvest is the only pass of the year that measures the result of every other pass. If the yield layer is not trustworthy at pass-to-pass resolution, the whole precision programme degrades to a guess with a map on it.

Do it with the grain tank empty at operating speed. Active Yield or manual calibration?

What you ran — 100% of 2025 Active Yield

Both machines ran Active Yield for every separator hour of 2025. It is automatic, hands-off, and explicitly a field totals solution. It rejects a load whenever machine pitch or roll exceeds 4°, the grain pile shifts, crop flow is inconsistent, you turn on a headland, or collection takes over 400 seconds. On rolling Koojan country with variable crop that is a lot of rejected loads, and the accepted ones average across whatever they happened to catch.

The change to make Manual, two-point

Deere names pass-to-pass accuracy as the reason to calibrate manually, and pass-to-pass is exactly what your prescription × as-applied × yield join needs. Turn Active Yield off first. Two points minimum, at different flow rates — one at normal productivity, one at about half — each at least 2270 kg, consistent flow, no headlands. Two points build the curve that holds accuracy as yield changes across the paddock.

Protein, and what it is actually for

HarvestLab 3000 on the clean grain elevator reads moisture and protein in wheat and barley (plus starch in barley), and moisture, protein and oil in canola, and writes them to Operations Center alongside yield. On its own that is a marketing number. Check yourself before the first round

Ten questions. Answer each one in your head, then open it. If more than two surprise you, the section they come from is worth a second read before harvest rather than during it.

You stop, lay a windrow and the tray under the back has a scatter of clean grain and a few canola pods that still rattle. What is the first move?

Neither the fan nor the sieve. Pods holding seed mean the rotor let them through, so it is a threshing problem — more rotor or a tighter concave. Wind the fan up to chase what looks like shoe loss and you blow the clean grain out the back as well.

X9 #1 comes off oats onto windrowed canola at four in the afternoon. What happens before the first full round?

Set the crop type in the display, swap to the BP15, and open the concave before you start — cereal clearance is low twenties, canola wants high thirties. Then drop a tray inside the first hour. In 2025 that one gap ran three days and read 13.2%.

Your mate tested his machine this morning and it came back at 0.8%. Does that tell you anything about yours?

No. Across the settled 2025 canola the two X9s sat five millimetres apart on concave, and in wheat nine millimetres apart. They do not converge on their own. Every machine gets its own tray.

The tray catch in canola is 120 g. Is that a disaster?

Not until you have aspirated it. Canola chaff and pod fragments are light and bulky, and on a 1 m pan in a 3 t/ha crop 9 g of clean seed is 1%, 27 g is 3%, 91 g is 10%. A 120 g catch that is a quarter seed is a 3.3% test, not a 13.2% one — and the two call for completely different responses.

The in-cab loss bars have been sitting at about a third all morning and you are happy. What have you actually learned?

Only that the loss has not changed since you last pressed Set to current. The bars are a change detector anchored to whatever you had at that instant, and a full display means roughly twice that. Anchor them to a bad number and they will read comfortable all day.

Wheat is threshing clean at 1300 rpm with the concave shut. Why change anything?

Because 1000 rpm with a 12 mm concave and three covers threshes it just as well, on Deere’s own figures, while drawing less power, burning less fuel, keeping the straw intact and leaving the shoe far less loaded. Geometry first, speed last.

Active tailings are showing two bars. Good or bad?

Neither — it means you have capacity in hand. Under three bars you can close the sieve or open the chaffer for throughput or a cleaner sample. It is 5+ bars that is the warning: grain in circulation, right-side shoe loss rising, more damage in the big seeds.

You are about to press Set Performance Target on a machine that feels good. Anything first?

Yes. Optimise by hand, drop the tray, get a real number, then set the target. Nothing in the automation loop measures grain on the ground, so a target set on an unoptimised machine is held all day, very competently.

Lupins go in for 2026 and the first paddock looks clean behind the machine. What does that tell you?

Almost nothing about the part that matters. The WA survey found lupins lost 12.4% in total — 11.0 points at the front and only 1.4 in the machine. A clean tray behind the combine fits a bad paddock in front of it. Run quadrat A in the standing crop and B in the stubble as well as the tray, and trust B minus A. Large seed makes loss invisible: nine seeds in a 0.1 m² frame is the 12.4%.

The knife hold-downs feel hot after a couple of minutes. What now?

Back them off. Hot means too tight, and over-tightening costs knife life. Reset to a 0.50 mm feeler or a business card, and never go below 0.30 mm even in tough cutting.

Five-minute loss check

  • Pick a representative run. Hold a steady ground speed and note the t/hr, not the km/h.
  • Chopper raised, chopper drive in neutral, straw curtain installed — Total Loss Mode.
  • Harvest 50–100 m, then stop and shut down. Do not reverse over the band.
  • Quadrat A in standing crop ahead of the header. Quadrat B in the stubble beside the band. Quadrat C in the band.
  • Measure the actual band width. Do not assume 1.7 m.
  • Winnow or aspirate each sample, then count or weigh.
  • Pre-harvest = A. Front loss = B − A. Machine loss = (C − B) × band ÷ 15.24.
  • Change one thing. Re-run. Two changes at once and you have learned nothing.
  • When the number is acceptable: press Set Performance Target, then Set to current on the loss display. In that order.

Crop changeover card

The one that cost real money in 2025

Every loss event this farm has actually measured happened in the first days of a new crop. Not mid-season, not in a hard patch — on the changeover. Both 2025 tray tests that returned a bad number were taken on a machine that had recently swapped crop, front, or both.

  • Before the first round. Set the crop type in the display. Harvest Settings Automation cannot help you if it thinks it is still in the last crop.
  • Open the concave first, then the rest. It is the setting that gets left behind. Cereal 10–20 mm; canola 30–40 mm on this farm's windrows. That is a bigger move than it feels like from the seat.
  • Front change counts as a crop change. Draper to BP15 alters how the mat feeds and the machine has to be re-learned around it.
  • Tray down inside the first hour. Not day three. Without a measured anchor the machine will search a 20 mm concave range for days and be confident the whole time — and one of those days is what 13.2% looks like.
  • Every machine, separately. The two X9s do not converge on the same settings by themselves.
  • Then press Set to current. Anchoring the loss bars to a measured-good machine is what makes them worth watching for the rest of the crop.
  • Any crop with no history — lupins, barley — gets a front check and a tray, early and again once the machine settles. WA lupin loss averaged 12.4%, and 11.0 points of it were lost at the front.

Canola pod squeeze test

Easy

Most of 10–15 pods break open in one squeeze and release seed. Run open and gentle — concave 30+, low rotor. Seed that shatters in the wind needs no help from the rotor.

Moderate

About half open, pods not crispy. Moderate separator aggression — concave around 20. Expect to go more aggressive once the dew sets in.

Hard

Very few break open. Run the most aggressive settings — concave 5–10 mm, rotor 900 rpm. Common in shatter-resistant varieties, high moisture, green stalk and where fungicide has held the plant alive.

Do this every paddock, and again after any frost, rain or heat event. Pod condition changes day to day, and it is the only input that tells you which of the three canola setting tables you should be on.
